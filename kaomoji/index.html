What Are Kaomoji?

Kaomoji (顔文字, literally "face characters") are Japanese-style text emoticons, born on Japanese computer networks around 1986 and typed today with Unicode characters. Unlike Western emoticons such as :-) that you read sideways, kaomoji face forward and use a creative mix of parentheses, letters, and special characters to express emotions — from joyful (ﾉ◕ヮ◕)ﾉ*:･ﾟ✧ to devastated (╥_╥).

Because kaomoji are plain Unicode text, they work on every platform that accepts text input — Discord, Twitter/X, WhatsApp, Telegram, TikTok, Instagram, and more. No app or special keyboard needed; a few faces with rare characters need a font that covers them.

Popular Kaomoji by Platform

Discord: Table flip (╯°□°）╯︵ ┻━┻, Shrug ¯\_(ツ)_/¯, and Lenny face ( ͡° ͜ʖ ͡°) are server classics. TikTok & Instagram: Cute kaomoji like (｡♥‿♥｡) and (≧◡≦) are often used in bios and comments. Twitter/X: UwU, OwO, and the bear ʕ•ᴥ•ʔ are familiar reactions.

Where Kaomoji Came From

Kaomoji were born on Japanese bulletin boards in the mid-1980s — the smiling (^_^) is usually traced to ASCII-NET users around 1986, a few years after Scott Fahlman proposed the Western :-) in 1982. The key difference is orientation: Western emoticons are read sideways, tilting your head to see the face, while kaomoji sit upright and face you directly. That upright design is why Japanese net culture could build such expressive faces — the eyes, mouth, and even arms all stay in their natural positions, so a single line of text can wave, cry, or flip a table.

How to Make Your Own Kaomoji

Most kaomoji follow one pattern: ( eye mouth eye ), with optional arms outside the brackets — though many famous faces, like UwU or ಠ_ಠ, skip the brackets. Swap the parts to change the mood. Common building blocks:

Eyes: ◕   ˘   ・   ●   >   ¬    Mouths: ‿   ᴥ   ω   ▽   _   ﻌ   ﮌ    Arms & effects: ﾉ   ╯   ┌   ୧   ✧   ♡   ✿

Start with (◕‿◕) for a happy face, drop the mouth to (◕_◕) for a blank stare, add arms for ヽ(◕‿◕)ﾉ celebrating. There is no "wrong" kaomoji — mixing brackets, katakana, and symbols is exactly how new ones are invented.

Kaomoji vs. Emoji vs. Emoticons

All three express feelings, but they work differently. An emoticon is basic ASCII like :-) or :D. An emoji is a single pictograph — 😊 — that your device's emoji font renders as a colored image, so it can look different on iPhone, Android, and Windows. A kaomoji is a sequence of ordinary text characters, so it stays text: it can be edited character by character, and copies cleanly into usernames and bios where emoji are sometimes blocked.

Do Kaomoji Work Everywhere?

Because kaomoji are plain Unicode text, they paste into almost any app — chat, bios, captions, code — with no special keyboard. Two things to know: on Discord, \ * and _ are formatting marks, so a face like the shrug ¯\_(ツ)_/¯ can lose part of an arm — wrapping the face in a code block (backticks) keeps every character as typed, and on very narrow phone screens the widest table-flip kaomoji may wrap onto two lines. If a rare character shows as a box, the device is simply missing that Unicode range — most faces on this page use common characters that current phones and browsers show, while a few rarer ones need a font with that range.

The Scripts Hiding Inside Kaomoji

Kaomoji look like pictures, but many are built from characters borrowed from real writing systems. We checked each of the 841 distinct faces in this library character by character against the Unicode database: 468 of them (56%) use at least one character that Unicode names after a non-Latin script — a letter, a mark, a digit or a punctuation sign, like the Katakana middle dot ・. Katakana is the most borrowed — it shows up in 172 faces.

That is why a face can look different from font to font, and why kaomoji are a small tour of the world's alphabets. The eyes in ಠ_ಠ are a Kannada letter, the arms in ٩(◕‿◕)۶ are an Arabic-Indic nine and an Extended Arabic-Indic six, and the bear in ʕ•ᴥ•ʔ is framed by two phonetic letters linguists use for throat sounds.
