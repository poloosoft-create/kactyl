What Are Kaomoji?

Kaomoji (顔文字, literally "face characters") are Japanese-style text emoticons built entirely from Unicode symbols. Unlike Western emoji that tilt sideways, kaomoji face forward and use a creative mix of parentheses, letters, and special characters to express emotions — from joyful (ﾉ◕ヮ◕)ﾉ*:･ﾟ✧ to devastated (╥_╥).

Because kaomoji are plain Unicode text, they work on every platform that accepts text input — Discord, Twitter/X, WhatsApp, Telegram, TikTok, Instagram, and more. No app, font, or special keyboard needed.

Popular Kaomoji by Platform

Discord: Table flip (╯°□°）╯︵ ┻━┻, Shrug ¯\_(ツ)_/¯, and Lenny face ( ͡° ͜ʖ ͡°) are server classics. TikTok & Instagram: Cute kaomoji like (｡♥‿♥｡) and (≧◡≦) dominate bios and comments. Twitter/X: UwU, OwO, and the bear ʕ•ᴥ•ʔ are mainstay reactions.

Where Kaomoji Came From

Kaomoji were born on Japanese bulletin boards in the mid-1980s — the smiling (^_^) is usually traced to ASCII-NET users around 1986, a few years after Scott Fahlman proposed the Western :-) in 1982. The key difference is orientation: Western emoticons are read sideways, tilting your head to see the face, while kaomoji sit upright and face you directly. That upright design is why Japanese net culture could build such expressive faces — the eyes, mouth, and even arms all stay in their natural positions, so a single line of text can wave, cry, or flip a table.

How to Make Your Own Kaomoji

Every kaomoji follows one template: ( eye mouth eye ), with optional arms outside the brackets. Swap the parts to change the mood. Common building blocks:

Eyes: ◕   ˘   ・   ●   ᴥ   >   ¬    Mouths: ‿   ω   ▽   _   ﻌ   ﮌ    Arms & effects: ﾉ   ╯   ┌   ୧   ✧   ♡   ✿

Start with (◕‿◕) for a happy face, drop the mouth to (◕_◕) for a blank stare, add arms for ヽ(◕‿◕)ﾉ celebrating. There is no "wrong" kaomoji — mixing brackets, katakana, and symbols is exactly how new ones are invented.

Kaomoji vs. Emoji vs. Emoticons

All three express feelings, but they work differently. An emoticon is basic ASCII like :-) or :D. An emoji is a single pictograph — 😊 — that your device's emoji font renders as a colored image, so it can look different on iPhone, Android, and Windows. A kaomoji is a sequence of ordinary text characters, so it never gets swapped for a font glyph: it looks identical everywhere, can be edited character by character, and copies cleanly into usernames and bios where emoji are sometimes blocked.

Do Kaomoji Work Everywhere?

Because kaomoji are plain Unicode text, they paste into almost any app — chat, bios, captions, code — with no special keyboard. Two things to know: on Discord, wrapping a complex kaomoji in a code block (backticks) preserves its exact spacing so a proportional font can't squash it, and on very narrow phone screens the widest table-flip kaomoji may wrap onto two lines. If a rare character shows as a box, the device is simply missing that Unicode range — every kaomoji on this page uses characters supported by all modern phones and browsers.

The Scripts Hiding Inside Kaomoji

Kaomoji look like pictures, but every one is a row of letters borrowed from real writing systems. We checked each of the 768 distinct faces in this library character by character against the Unicode database: 421 of them (55%) use at least one letter or mark from a non-Latin script. Katakana is the most borrowed — it shows up in 149 faces.

That is why a face can look different from font to font, and why kaomoji are a small tour of the world's alphabets. The eyes in ಠ_ಠ are a Kannada letter, the arms in ٩(◕‿◕)۶ are an Arabic-Indic nine and an Extended Arabic-Indic six, and the bear in ʕ•ᴥ•ʔ is framed by two phonetic letters linguists use for throat sounds.
