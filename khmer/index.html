Khmer has been written for at least 1,400 years. The script developed from the Pallava script of South India, and the oldest dated inscription in Khmer, K. 557/600 from Angkor Borei, was carved in 611 CE. Through the Angkor period the same script covered temple walls across the empire, and it went on to shape the Thai and Lao alphabets. Khmer is written in two main styles: the rounded Aksar Mul, used for titles and inscriptions, and the slanted Aksar Chrieng of everyday writing. Guinness World Records lists Khmer as the longest alphabet in use, with 74 letters: 33 consonants, 23 dependent vowels and 12 independent vowels. Khmer was added to Unicode in version 3.0 in 1999, and in 2000 the Cambodian government set up a committee with the National ICT Development Authority (NiDA) to standardise Khmer on computers, which produced the NiDA keyboard layout. About 19 million people speak Khmer as their first language.

Khmer stacks consonants. When two consonants meet with no vowel between them, the second is written under the first as a subscript “foot”, coeng: the name ខ្មែរ itself has one (ខ with ម underneath). On a keyboard that means typing the first letter, the invisible sign ្, then the second letter. Khmer also has two series of consonants that change how a vowel sounds, so កា is “ka” but គា is “kea”, and vowel signs can sit before, above, below or after a consonant. Words are written without spaces; a space marks the end of a phrase and ។ ends a sentence.

A lot of Khmer text is still not Unicode. Legacy fonts such as Limon, where Latin characters are drawn to look like Khmer letters, remain common in offices and print; the text turns into Latin nonsense on any device without the font, and search engines cannot read it. On phones the hardest part is the subscripts: stacking a consonant with coeng takes several taps, so many people chat in Khmer written with Latin letters instead. Cambodia also uses its own digits, ០ to ៩, in everyday life, which most English keyboards cannot type. Large Khmer-speaking communities live in the United States, France and Australia.
