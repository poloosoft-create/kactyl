About the Japanese Script

Japanese is written with three scripts at once, and each arrived differently. Kanji — logographic characters — were borrowed from China around the 5th century, the first way Japanese was ever written. Because Chinese characters fit the Japanese language poorly, scribes then invented two native syllabaries from them. Hiragana grew out of the flowing cursive forms of man'yōgana during the Heian period (9th–10th century) — at first called "women's hand" — while katakana was pieced together by Buddhist monks from small fragments of kanji used as reading aids. Both settled into 46 basic characters each, a perfect sound-for-sound mirror. Modern kanji use is anchored by the Ministry of Education's jōyō list of 2,136 characters, last revised on 30 November 2010. The result is a system where a single sentence routinely blends all three — something no other living language does.

What Makes the Japanese Alphabet Distinctive

These are the features of the Japanese script that trip up learners coming from the Latin alphabet — the things worth understanding before you start reading or typing:

  • Japanese writes with three scripts at once: hiragana (rounded, for grammar and native words), katakana (angular, for foreign words), and kanji (Chinese characters, for meaning). This chart is hiragana, the first one learners master.
  • Hiragana is a syllabary, not an alphabet: each symbol is a whole syllable — か is “ka”, not “k” + “a”. There are 46 basic syllables.
  • Hiragana and katakana are two costumes for the same 46 sounds — か and カ both say “ka” — kept apart only by the job they do.
  • Two small dots (dakuten ゛) or a circle (handakuten ゜) shift the sound: か (ka) → が (ga), は (ha) → ば (ba) → ぱ (pa).
  • ん is the only standalone consonant, and を (pronounced “o”) now survives only as the particle that marks a sentence’s object.

Japanese Script — The Facts

  • Unicode block: Hiragana U+3040–309F; Katakana U+30A0–30FF
  • ISO 15924 script code: Hira (410) / Jpan (413)
  • Script family: Syllabary (kana), derived from simplified kanji
  • First attested: ~9th century CE (Heian period)
  • Speakers: 125 million (Ethnologue)

Sources: Unicode Hiragana block (U+3040) · Ethnologue — Japanese · ISO 15924 — Hira

Frequently Asked Questions

How does Romaji mode turn English letters into Japanese?+
Turn on Romaji (Roman) mode, type phonetically, and press Space — the keyboard converts the word to kana the way a phone IME does. "ka"→か, "shi"→し, "tsu"→つ, "kya"→きゃ, and a double consonant like "tt" builds the small っ. It's a full wapuro-style converter, not a lookup of a few words.
What's the difference between hiragana and katakana?+
They spell the same 46 sounds but do different jobs. Hiragana (ひらがな) writes native Japanese words, particles and verb endings; katakana (カタカナ) writes foreign loanwords, brand names and sound effects. コーヒー (coffee) is katakana; こんにちは (hello) is hiragana.
Do I need to install a Japanese IME to use this?+
No. Everything runs in your browser. Tap the kana grid or use Romaji mode, then Copy and paste. Nothing is installed and no system language settings change — ideal on a borrowed phone, a library PC or a work laptop.
How do I type katakana and small kana like っ ゃ ゅ ょ?+
Open the special-characters panel for the katakana set and the small kana (っ ゃ ゅ ょ ぁ ぃ...). In Romaji mode they're automatic: "kk/tt"→っ, "kya/shu/cho"→きゃ/しゅ/ちょ.
