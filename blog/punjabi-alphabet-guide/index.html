About the Punjabi Script

Gurmukhi was standardized in the 16th century by Guru Angad, the second Sikh Guru — the name means 'from the mouth of the Guru'. It descends from the Landa and Sharada scripts of the Brahmi family, and it is the script of the Guru Granth Sahib, compiled in 1604. The core alphabet is the painti — 'the thirty-five' — later extended with six dotted pair-bindi letters (ਸ਼ ਖ਼ ਗ਼ ਜ਼ ਫ਼ ਲ਼) to write Persian and Arabic loan sounds. Punjabi is a two-script language: India writes Gurmukhi, Pakistan writes the same language in Shahmukhi (Arabic script). This keyboard types Gurmukhi. Gurmukhi's ordering is also unusual: it begins with the vowel bearers and ਸ ਹ before the classical consonant rows — an arrangement credited to the Gurus' pedagogical redesign, made to teach literacy fast to ordinary people rather than scholars.

What Makes the Punjabi Alphabet Distinctive

These are the features of the Punjabi script that trip up learners coming from the Latin alphabet — the things worth understanding before you start reading or typing:

  • Punjabi is written in Gurmukhi — “from the Guru’s mouth” — the script standardised by the second Sikh Guru, Angad, in the 16th century to record the Sikh scriptures.
  • The 35 core letters are called the Painti (literally “thirty-five”) and are learned in a fixed traditional order, like a song.
  • Punjabi is tonal — rare for an Indo-Aryan language. The historically voiced-aspirate letters (ਘ ਝ ਢ ਧ ਭ) and ਹ shift the pitch of a syllable, so tone can change a word’s meaning.
  • Modern Gurmukhi adds dotted letters — ਸ਼ (sha), ਜ਼ (za), ਫ਼ (fa), ਖ਼ (kha), ਗ਼ (gha) — for sounds that came in with Persian and English words.
  • Like other Brahmic scripts it’s an abugida with a top line, but Gurmukhi’s letters have a distinctive squared, upright shape.

  • ISO 15924 script code: Guru (310)
  • Script family: Brahmic abugida (Gurmukhi, 16th century)
  • First attested: 16th century CE (standardised by Guru Angad)

Sources: Unicode Gurmukhi block (U+0A00) · Ethnologue — Punjabi · ISO 15924 — Guru

Did You Know? Punjabi is a tonal language — one of the very few Indo-European languages with contrastive tones. The three tones (high-falling, low-rising, and level) are written using the letters ਹ and ਵ in unusual positions within words, a unique orthographic system with no parallel in other South Asian languages.

About the Punjabi Language

Punjabi is spoken by 125 million people across Pakistan (using Shahmukhi/Arabic script) and India (using Gurmukhi script). Indian Punjabi uses Gurmukhi — the script of the Guru Granth Sahib, the holy scripture of Sikhism. The Punjabi diaspora in the UK (especially Birmingham and Wolverhampton), Canada (Vancouver, Toronto), and the US is one of the most culturally cohesive South Asian communities.

Gurmukhi — The Script of the Guru Granth Sahib

Gurmukhi script was standardized by Guru Angad Dev in the 16th century for the Sikh community to read and share the sacred compositions of the Gurus. The Guru Granth Sahib — the eternal Guru of Sikhism — is written entirely in Gurmukhi. For Sikhs, the script carries deep spiritual significance. In the diaspora, Gurmukhi literacy is actively taught at gurdwaras and Punjabi schools. The script has 35 primary letters and several vowel markers (lagaan matras). Unlike Devanagari, Gurmukhi has a consistent one-letter-one-sound correspondence, making it phonetically regular. The Punjabi diaspora's vibrant Bhangra music culture, which went global in the 1990s through UK Bhangra and later through Bollywood and international collaborations, keeps Punjabi culturally present worldwide.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does this keyboard type Gurmukhi or Shahmukhi?+
Gurmukhi — the script of Indian Punjab, Sikh scripture and Punjabi-language schooling in India. After the 1947 partition, written Punjabi split with the border: Pakistan writes the same language in Shahmukhi, an Arabic-script system, while India kept Gurmukhi as the official script of Punjab state. Everything you type here is standard Gurmukhi Unicode (the U+0A00 block), so it's searchable, copy-safe and renders on every modern device. If you need Shahmukhi, an Urdu-style keyboard is the right tool — the two scripts share no letters.
How do I type standalone vowels in Gurmukhi?+
Gurmukhi vowels can't stand alone at the start of a word — each rides one of three bearer letters, and the pairing is fixed: ੳ carries ਉ, ਊ and ਓ; ਅ carries ਆ, ਐ and ਔ (and is itself the 'a' sound); ੲ carries ਇ, ਈ and ਏ. So ਇਹ (this) is ੲ + ਿ, and ਊਠ (camel) is ੳ + ੂ. After a consonant you skip the bearer and attach the matra directly: ਕ + ੀ = ਕੀ. The keyboard shows the bearers on the layout, and phonetic mode picks the right one automatically.
How do I type the dotted letters ਸ਼, ਖ਼, ਜ਼, ਫ਼?+
The dotted letters — pair bindi, 'dot below' — extend the original 35-letter painti with sounds Punjabi absorbed from Persian, Arabic and English: ਸ਼ (sh) as in ਸ਼ਹਿਰ (city), ਖ਼ (kh) as in ਖ਼ਬਰ (news), ਗ਼ (gh), ਜ਼ (z) as in ਜ਼ਰੂਰੀ (necessary), ਫ਼ (f) as in ਫ਼ੋਨ (phone), plus ਲ਼ — a native retroflex 'l' that many keyboards forget entirely. All six have their own keys here. Writing ਜਰੂਰੀ without the dot is a common spelling slip; the dot is what makes it standard written Punjabi.
What is the addak (ੱ) and when do I use it?+
The addak sits above the line BEFORE a consonant and doubles it — and Punjabi doubles consonants far more than Hindi, so it earns a dedicated key. ਪੱਕਾ (pakka — firm), ਸੱਚ (sach — truth), ਹੱਥ (hath — hand), ਚੱਲ (chal — let's go). It changes meaning, not just pronunciation: ਪਤਾ means 'address, known' while ਪੱਤਾ means 'leaf'. If your Punjabi text looks right but reads slightly off to a native speaker, a missing addak is the usual suspect.
