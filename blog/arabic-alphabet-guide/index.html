About the Arabic Script

Arabic script emerged from Aramaic Nabataean cursive around 4th century CE, with the Namara inscription (328 CE) among earliest evidence. Full standardization occurred through Islamic calligraphy development from 7th century onward. By 10th century, Arabic script was completely standardized through scholarly refinement by calligraphers like Ibn Muqla. Diacritical marks (tashkeel) were formalized for Quranic texts. Medieval periods saw regional variations, but Ottoman standardization (15th-19th centuries) reinforced modern conventions. Contemporary Arabic orthography remains stable since pre-Islamic standardization.

What Makes the Arabic Alphabet Distinctive

These are the features of the Arabic script that trip up learners coming from the Latin alphabet — the things worth understanding before you start reading or typing:

  • All 28 Arabic letters are consonants. Short vowels (a, i, u) are written as small marks above or below the letter — called ḥarakāt — and are normally left out of everyday writing, so readers supply them from context.
  • Arabic is fully cursive: almost every letter changes shape depending on where it falls in a word — isolated, initial, medial, or final — giving up to four forms of the same letter (the IMFI forms).
  • Six letters never join to the letter that follows them — ا, د, ذ, ر, ز, و — creating a deliberate break in the cursive flow, which is the first thing that surprises learners.
  • Fourteen 'sun letters' assimilate the l of the definite article ال: al-shams ('the sun') is actually pronounced ash-shams, while the 'moon letters' keep the l audible (al-qamar).
  • Four emphatic consonants — ص, ض, ط, ظ — are pronounced with the back of the tongue raised toward the throat and have no English equivalent. Arabic is even nicknamed lughat al-ḍād, 'the language of the ḍād (ض),' after a sound long believed unique to it.
  • The letters ع (ʿayn) and غ (ghayn) are produced deep in the throat, and ء (hamza) marks a glottal stop that rides on a carrier letter (أ إ ؤ ئ) rather than standing on its own.

About the Arabic Language

Arabic is a right-to-left language used by 420 million people across 26 countries. In North Africa, Franco Arabic (Arabizi) is the dominant informal digital writing style, blending Latin letters and numbers to represent Arabic sounds. WhatsApp is the primary messaging platform across all Arab markets, with Ramadan and Eid generating massive spikes in Arabic messaging traffic.

Franco Arabic — The North African Digital Code

Franco Arabic, also called Arabizi, emerged in the early 2000s as young Algerians, Moroccans, and Tunisians began texting and chatting online using Latin-script phones that had no Arabic keyboard. They invented a system where numbers represent Arabic sounds that don't exist in Latin: 3 for ع (ayn), 7 for ح (ha), 9 for ق (qaf), 5 for خ (kha). Today, millions of North Africans communicate digitally in this hybrid code. Kactyl's Franco mode bridges both worlds: type in Franco Arabic and output proper Arabic Unicode script. Frequently Asked Questions

What are IMFI letter forms?+
IMFI = Isolated, Medial, Final, Initial — the four positions a letter occupies in a word. Each Arabic letter appears in up to 4 completely different shapes depending on position.
Why is Arabic hard to type?+
Position-dependent letter forms (IMFI), diacritical marks stacked horizontally on consonants, right-to-left directionality, and optional vowels create typing complexity absent in Latin scripts.
What is tashkeel?+
Tashkeel (vowel marks: fatha, damma, kasra, sukun) clarifies pronunciation and distinguishes meanings. Modern casual writing omits tashkeel; formal/academic/religious texts include it.
How many Arabic speakers are there?+
422-491 million native speakers across 22 Arabic-speaking nations. It ranks among the world's top 3 most-spoken languages by native speakers.
