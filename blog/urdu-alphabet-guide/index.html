About the Urdu Script

Urdu grew out of the Hindustani spoken around Delhi and took its written form in the Perso-Arabic script during the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal era — the name comes from the Turkic ordu ("camp"), short for zaban-e-urdu-e-mualla, "the language of the exalted camp." Its visual identity is Nastaliq, the "hanging" calligraphic style perfected in 14th–15th century Persia and credited to Mir Ali Tabrizi, which Urdu made its standard while Arabic kept the flatter Naskh. Declared Pakistan's national language in 1947 (and one of India's 22 scheduled languages), Urdu struggled digitally for decades: Nastaliq's diagonal cascade was so hard to render that computers displayed Urdu in Arabic-style Naskh, and the publishing world ran on InPage (1994) until modern Nastaliq web fonts finally brought the authentic style online.

What Makes the Urdu Alphabet Distinctive

These are the features of the Urdu script that trip up learners coming from the Latin alphabet — the things worth understanding before you start reading or typing:

  • Urdu is written in the Perso-Arabic script, extending Arabic and Persian with extra letters for South Asian sounds — and it is traditionally set in the flowing, diagonal Nastaʿlīq calligraphic style, which is why Urdu looks different from Arabic even where the letters are the same.
  • The retroflex consonants ٹ (ṭ), ڈ (ḍ), ڑ (ṛ) — made by curling the tongue back — mark words of Indic origin and exist in neither Arabic nor Persian.
  • Aspiration has its own letter: the “two-eyed” he ھ (do-chashmī he) is placed after a consonant to make an aspirated pair — کھ (kh), گھ (gh), بھ (bh) — a whole series of sounds.
  • ں (nūn ghunna) marks a nasalized vowel rather than a full n, and ے (baṛī ye) is a distinct final e/ai letter, separate from the ordinary ی.
  • As in Arabic and Persian, short vowels are usually unwritten and the script is cursive, with letters changing shape by position.

Urdu Script — The Facts

  • Unicode block: Arabic, U+0600–06FF (+ Arabic Extended-A)
  • ISO 15924 script code: Arab (160), Nastaʿlīq style
  • Script family: Perso-Arabic abjad, extended for Urdu
  • First attested: ~13th–14th century CE (Hindavi/Urdu in Perso-Arabic script)
  • Speakers: 246 million (Ethnologue)

Sources: Unicode Arabic block (U+0600) · Ethnologue — Urdu · ISO 15924 — Arab

Did You Know? Urdu and Hindi are mutually intelligible in spoken form but look completely different in writing — Hindi uses Devanagari (left-to-right) and Urdu uses Nastaliq (right-to-left). They share the same grammar but use different vocabulary for formal registers, making them linguistically fascinating sister languages.

About the Urdu Language

Urdu is the national language of Pakistan with 230 million speakers. It uses the Nastaliq calligraphic style — considered the most beautiful script in the world — which flows diagonally right-to-left. Pakistani diaspora communities in the UK, US, Canada, and Middle East use WhatsApp, Facebook, and YouTube extensively in Urdu for family communication.

Nastaliq — The World's Most Beautiful Script

Urdu's Nastaliq script is not just a writing system — it's a form of art. Unlike the more rectangular Naskh style used for Arabic, Nastaliq flows in a diagonal, calligraphic manner that Urdu speakers consider deeply tied to their cultural identity. Typing Urdu digitally was historically challenging because Nastaliq rendering requires sophisticated font technology. Today, Unicode Nastaliq fonts like Jameel Noori Nastaleeq display correctly on most modern devices. Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Urdu look flat and "Arabic-style" in some apps?+
That's the font, not your typing. Authentic Urdu renders in Nastaliq — words cascade diagonally and letters stack — while many apps fall back to Arabic Naskh fonts that sit flat on the baseline. Text typed here is standard Unicode, so it displays in whatever font the destination app uses; on this page you see it in a proper Nastaliq-style font.
Can I type Roman Urdu and get Urdu script?+
Yes — that's the phonetic mode. Type "kaise ho" the way you already write in WhatsApp and it becomes کیسے ہو. Roman Urdu is how most Pakistanis type day-to-day, so the keyboard treats it as a first-class input method rather than forcing you to memorize the Urdu layout.
What's the difference between an Urdu keyboard and an Arabic keyboard?+
Urdu has letters Arabic simply doesn't: the retroflexes ٹ ڈ ڑ (marked with a small ط on top), do-chashmi he (ھ) for aspirated sounds, noon ghunna (ں), yeh barree (ے), and گ چ پ ژ inherited from Persian. On an Arabic keyboard those keys don't exist, so words like ٹھیک ("okay") can't even be typed.
How is Urdu different from Persian to type?+
Urdu builds on the Persian alphabet (including پ چ ژ گ) and then adds its own layer: retroflex ٹ ڈ ڑ for sounds Persian doesn't have, do-chashmi he (ھ) to mark aspiration, noon ghunna (ں) for nasal vowels, and yeh barree (ے). Persian also normally renders in Naskh, while Urdu's standard is Nastaliq.
