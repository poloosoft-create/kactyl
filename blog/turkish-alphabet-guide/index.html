About the Turkish Script

Turkish switched from Arabic/Persian script (Ottoman Turkish) in 1928 when Atatürk adopted the Latin alphabet as part of the Turkish National Movement's modernization reforms. The Ottoman Empire had used an Arabic-derived script for 600+ years. 1928 marked the official adoption of a 29-letter Latin alphabet modified specifically for Turkish, replacing the previous 108-letter Arabic-script repertoire. The Turkish Latin alphabet added diacritical marks (ç, ğ, ı, ö, ş, ü) to Latin to represent Turkish phonemes absent in standard Latin. This 1928 reform was one of the most rapid and complete language transformations in modern history — implemented in less than a year. Modern Turkish orthography has remained stable since 1928.

What Makes the Turkish Alphabet Distinctive

These are the features of the Turkish script that trip up learners coming from the Latin alphabet — the things worth understanding before you start reading or typing:

  • Turkish’s most famous quirk is the dotted vs dotless i: İ/i (front, “ee”) and I/ı (back, “uh”) are two different letters, and the dot is kept even in capitals — İstanbul. This is the origin of the classic “Turkish-i” software bug.
  • There is no Q, W or X — their jobs are done by K, V and KS.
  • Ğ (soft g) is never pronounced on its own; it just lengthens the vowel before it, so dağ sounds like “daa”.
  • The six vowels form front/back pairs (e/a, i/ı, ö/o, ü/u), and vowel harmony makes suffix vowels change to match the word — a defining feature of Turkish.
  • The letters ç, ş, ö, ü, ğ, ı were introduced in 1928, when Turkey switched from the Arabic script to this Latin alphabet in Atatürk’s reform.

Turkey's Script Revolution — 1928 and the Latin Alphabet

On November 1, 1928, the Republic of Turkey switched from Arabic script to a modified Latin alphabet in one of history's most dramatic language reforms. The change was driven by Mustafa Kemal Atatürk's goal of modernizing Turkey and breaking with the Ottoman past. Citizens had 90 days to learn the new script. Books, signs, newspapers — everything changed simultaneously. The reform transformed Turkish from a language written right-to-left in Arabic script to a phonetically precise left-to-right Latin system. Modern Turkish has 29 letters including 6 unique to Turkish: Ç (ch), Ğ (soft g, often silent), İ (dotted i), Ö (umlauted o), Ş (sh), Ü (umlauted u). Note the crucial distinction: İ (dotted capital i) and I (undotted capital i) are different letters in Turkish — I lowercases to ı, İ lowercases to i. This confuses spellcheckers in other languages.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did Turkey switch to Latin alphabet in 1928?+
Atatürk's 1928 alphabet reform was part of Turkey's modernization movement to align with Western Europe and break from the Ottoman Islamic past. The Latin alphabet was seen as more efficient, easier to teach to illiterate populations, and symbolically important for Turkey's new secular identity.
What are the special Turkish letters?+
Turkish has 6 unique letters absent from standard Latin: ç (soft c), ğ (soft g), ı (dotless i), ö (o-umlaut), ş (s-cedilla), ü (u-umlaut). Each is phonemically distinct.
What is vowel harmony in Turkish?+
Turkish suffixes change their vowels to match the root word's vowel type. Example: -ler suffix for front-vowel words, -lar suffix for back-vowel words. This creates a flowing cascading quality to Turkish morphology.
Why is the dotless ı different from i?+
In Turkish, ı (no dot) = /ɯ/ and i (with dot) = /i/. They're completely separate letters representing different sounds. Confusing them changes word meanings entirely.
