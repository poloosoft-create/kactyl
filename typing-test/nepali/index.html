Preeti, Unicode Traditional, Romanized — and the habit that breaks

Nepali has the sharpest version of a problem the other scripts only hint at. The two dominant typing systems do not just use different keys; they ask you to type the same word in a different order.

Preeti dates from the early 1990s, built for DOS and early Windows. It reassigns every QWERTY key to a Devanagari glyph, and the file underneath holds ASCII, not Nepali. Remove the font and your document is meaningless Latin text. It is also, for historical reasons, the system long associated with government work and Loksewa preparation, which is why it refuses to disappear.

Unicode Traditional keeps a familiar feel but fixes the encoding, storing real Devanagari codepoints in the range U+0900–U+097F. Here is the part that catches people: to write किसान in Preeti you type ि first and then क, matching what you see. In Unicode you type क first and then ि, and the script does the reordering. Everything else can transfer between the two systems; that habit cannot. It is the single most common reason an experienced Preeti typist feels clumsy on a Unicode keyboard.

Romanized input converts as you type — namaste becomes नमस्ते — and is how most people type Nepali on a phone.

This test measures Unicode. If you type Preeti, your speed here will not reflect your real ability, because you are fighting a reordering rule rather than typing.

Retraining a habit is different from building one

If you have never typed Nepali, skip this section — you are building a habit and the ordinary advice applies. If you type Preeti and are moving to Unicode, you are doing something harder, and it is worth naming: you are not learning, you are overwriting. The matra order inverts. Every time you reach for ि before its consonant, a habit that has been correct for years produces an error.

Overwriting a habit responds badly to speed practice and well to slow, deliberate repetition. Type at half your comfortable speed for a while, specifically on words carrying ि, ी and े, and let the new order become the automatic one before you ask for pace back. Practising fast simply rehearses the old reflex under pressure.

Beyond that, Nepali asks more of you than its word count suggests. This list uses 340 distinct letter shapes — the widest inventory of any language tested here, wider than Hindi or Marathi over the same number of words. More shapes means more sequences that have to become automatic, and it is the honest reason Nepali typing takes longer to learn than people expect.

Why the Preeti question is worth settling before you practise

Preeti's hold on Nepali office work is a genuine dilemma rather than simple inertia. If the office you are joining runs on Preeti documents, Preeti is the practical skill. If you are writing anything that leaves that office — a web page, an email, a form, anything that needs to be searched or read on a phone — Preeti text will not survive the trip, because it was never Nepali to begin with.

The reordering difference makes this more than an encoding preference. Every hour spent building speed in one system is partly training a habit the other system will punish. It is worth deciding which one you actually need before putting the hours in, rather than splitting them and being mediocre at both.

Nepali also has the widest letter inventory of the six languages tested here — 340 distinct shapes across 600 words, more than Hindi or Marathi over the same sample size. More shapes means more keys and combinations to become fluent in, which is a real reason Nepali typing takes longer to learn than the word count suggests.

Where Nepali typists lose time

ि

The reordering trap

The one habit that does not transfer. Preeti types it before the consonant, Unicode after. If you learned Preeti, this will be your most frequent error here.

्

Half letters in dense words

96% of the words in this list contain a cluster costing more keystrokes than it displays. छन्, गर्न and भएको are ordinary Nepali and all of them stack.

ँ

Candrabindu

Nepali uses ँ more than Hindi does, and it sits where Hindi practice material rarely sends you.

ऋ

Vocalic r and its matra

ऋ and ृ appear in formal Nepali vocabulary and are easy to confuse at speed.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Does this test work with Preeti?
No, and it is important to understand why rather than assume it is a bug. Preeti produces Latin characters that a Preeti font draws as Devanagari. The page receives Latin. There is no Nepali there to measure.
How is the speed calculated?
Correctly typed whole words per minute, with visual letter units per minute alongside. On this word list that convention would report roughly 1.8 times the words-per-minute figure shown here, because it counts the keystrokes hidden inside conjuncts as if they were separate letters.
I type fast in Preeti but slowly here. Why?
Most likely the matra reordering. In Preeti you type ि before its consonant; in Unicode you type it after. That is one habit, and it fires on a large share of Nepali words.
Do I need Nepali installed?
No. The on-screen Devanagari keyboard below can be clicked.
Is this useful for Loksewa preparation?
For speed and accuracy in Unicode Nepali, yes. If your exam specifies Preeti, practise in the software the exam uses — the skills only partly overlap.
Where do the words come from?
A Nepali news corpus, frequency-ordered, with Hindi function words filtered out so that quoted Hindi in the source text does not leak into a Nepali test.
Why is Nepali harder to type than Hindi?
Wider letter inventory. 340 distinct shapes across 600 words, and words averaging 5.47 codepoints.
What is the longest word in the list?
कोरोनाभाइरस, at 7 visual units.
Is Romanized typing measured?
If your Romanized input produces Unicode Nepali, yes — the test reads the output, not the method.
Does it work on a phone?
Yes, with your phone keyboard, which will usually be Romanized.
Is my result stored?
Your personal best stays in your browser and is never sent to us.
Should I learn Preeti or Unicode?
Depends entirely on where your typing has to go. Inside a Preeti office, Preeti. Anywhere that text must be searched, sent or read on a phone, Unicode.
