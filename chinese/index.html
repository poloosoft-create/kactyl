Chinese is the oldest writing system still in daily use, with an unbroken line back more than three thousand years to characters carved on oracle bones. Unlike an alphabet, it is logographic: each character stands for a word or idea rather than a sound. Reference dictionaries record over 54,000 characters, yet everyday literacy rests on roughly 3,500, and China's adult literacy rate now sits near 97%. In the 20th century the writing was reshaped twice for accessibility — the mainland's simplified characters reduced the stroke count of many common forms, while Taiwan and Hong Kong kept the traditional shapes. Then, in 1958, the government adopted Hanyu Pinyin — the Roman-letter spelling system devised under Zhou Youguang — to teach pronunciation and drive literacy. Pinyin is why a billion people can type thousands of characters from a plain QWERTY keyboard today.

Chinese has no alphabet — it writes with thousands of individual characters, so the real question for any keyboard is how you reach them. This one gives you a grid of the most common characters to tap directly, plus a true Pinyin input mode: type the romanized sound and a candidate bar shows the matching characters ranked by frequency, so you pick 你 for ni and 好 for hao to build 你好 — the exact workflow of every phone and desktop IME in China. Tones are what make the sounds meaningful: the syllable ma alone spans 妈 (mother), 麻 (hemp), 马 (horse) and 骂 (scold) depending on pitch.

In China, WeChat is not just a chat app — it's the operating system of daily life, with over 1.38 billion monthly users as of 2024 handling messages, payments, mini-programs and official accounts. Weibo, Xiaohongshu (RED) and Douyin fill the rest of the screen, and every one of them needs Chinese input. Outside mainland China the picture flips: a diaspora student on a borrowed laptop, a traveler at a hotel PC, or anyone on a phone set to English suddenly has no Chinese IME installed and no simple way to add one. A browser keyboard closes that gap instantly — tap common characters or type Pinyin, choose from the candidate bar, copy, and paste clean Chinese into WeChat, Weibo, TikTok, email or a form, with nothing installed and no system settings touched.

Inside the Character System

Chinese doesn't spell words from sounds — it builds them from thousands of meaning-carrying characters, four tones and a set of shared roots. Here's how it fits together.

~3,500 everyday characters4 tonesPinyin since 19581.1B speakers
Meaning, not sound

Hanzi 汉字

人山水火

Each character is a whole word or idea, not a letter. Dictionaries list over 54,000, but around 3,500 cover everyday reading — and China's adult literacy sits near 97%. 人 is "person", 山 "mountain", drawn from the shapes they once pictured.

中国 — China  ·  学生 — student
Pitch is meaning

The four tones

妈麻马骂

The same syllable ma means four different things depending on pitch: mā 妈 mother, má 麻 hemp, mǎ 马 horse, mà 骂 to scold. Tone isn't decoration — it's part of the word.

妈 mā  ·  麻 má  ·  马 mǎ  ·  骂 mà
The building blocks

Radicals 部首

氵木口心

Most characters are built from smaller roots that hint at meaning: 氵 (water) sits in 河 river and 海 sea; 木 (tree) in 林 forest; 口 (mouth) in 吃 eat. Learn the radicals and characters stop looking random.

氵 + 每 → 海 (sea)
The keyboard bridge

Pinyin 拼音

nǐhǎo

Romanization officially adopted in 1958 (architect Zhou Youguang) — it's how the whole country types. Our Pinyin mode works the same way: type ni, pick 你 from the candidate bar; type hao, pick 好 → 你好.

nǐ → 你  ·  hǎo → 好  ·  你好 hello
