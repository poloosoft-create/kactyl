Somali is one of the world's youngest standardized written languages — the official orthography dates only to October 1972. For half a century before that, Somalia fought its famous "script wars": religious scholars wrote Somali in Arabic script (Wadaad writing), nationalists championed the invented Osmanya alphabet created by Osman Yusuf Kenadid in the 1920s, and modernizers pushed Latin. The government finally adopted the Latin system engineered by linguist Shire Jama Ahmed, then sent thousands of students into the countryside in the 1974–75 rural literacy campaign — one of Africa's most ambitious. All of it built on one of the continent's richest oral traditions: Somali gabay poetry, composed and memorized for centuries without writing.

Somali needs no special script — but typing it on an English keyboard is quietly miserable. Autocorrect constantly "fixes" Somali words into English ones, doubled vowels and doubled consonants both change meaning (so every letter counts), and the apostrophe marking the glottal stop gets mangled by smart-quote settings. The alphabet drops p, v and z entirely, and leans on digraphs — dh, kh and sh carry sounds single letters can't. This keyboard treats those digraphs as single one-tap keys, keeps predictions Somali-aware instead of English-biased, and never rewrites your words behind your back.

Somali is spoken by 22M+ people across Somalia, Djibouti, Ethiopia's Somali Region and northeastern Kenya — and by one of the most dispersed diasporas on earth. Minneapolis's "Little Mogadishu" is North America's largest Somali community; London, Toronto and Nairobi's Eastleigh district anchor the rest. That dispersion makes typing infrastructure matter: remittances of roughly $1.5–2 billion a year flow home through relationships maintained daily on WhatsApp and Facebook, and Somali TikTok has boomed since 2020 — captions, comments and status updates all typed by people on borrowed devices, shared phones and desktop browsers where installing a keyboard isn't an option.

How Somali Spells Its Sounds

Somalia adopted this Latin alphabet in 1972 — and immediately bent ordinary letters to Cushitic sounds no European language has.

Latin since 1972Doubled long vowelsc = ʿayndh retroflex
Recycled letters

c and x aren’t what you think

CaliXamarCumar

Somali gave spare Latin letters new jobs: c is the deep throat sound ʿayn — Cali is Ali, Cumar is Omar — and x is the heavy h: Xamar is Mogadishu’s own name. Read them the English way and every Somali name comes out wrong; this keyboard’s layout keeps both one tap away.

Cali — Ali · Xamar — Mogadishu
Length is meaning

Long vowels are written twice

ooaaee

Vowel length changes words in Somali, so the spelling always doubles a long vowel — Soomaaliya carries two of them in its own name. No guessing, no diacritics: what you see is how long you say it.

Soomaaliya — Somalia
The signature sound

dh — the retroflex d

dhdhul

The digraph dh marks a retroflex d — tongue curled back — in everyday words like dhul (land) and dhar (clothes). It’s one of the sounds that instantly identifies Somali speech.

dhul — land · dhar — clothes

A young alphabet, a huge campaign

1972

After decades of debate between Arabic script and the homegrown Osmanya alphabet, Somalia chose Latin in October 1972 — then launched one of Africa’s largest literacy campaigns to teach it nationwide.
