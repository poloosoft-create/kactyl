Armenian has used the same alphabet for more than 1,600 years. Around 405 AD the monk and scholar Mesrop Mashtots created a script of 36 letters so that the Bible and church services could be read in Armenian rather than Greek or Syriac. Two more letters, օ (o) and ֆ (f), were added in the Middle Ages, giving the 38 letters of the modern alphabet. In the 1920s, Soviet Armenia reformed the spelling of Eastern Armenian: among other changes, the ligature և came to be counted as a letter of its own. Armenians in the diaspora who speak Western Armenian, and the Armenian community of Iran, kept the older classical spelling, so the same word can still be written two ways today. The letters themselves never changed: a text from the 5th century and a text message from Yerevan use the same shapes. Armenian was part of the very first version of Unicode in 1991, with its own block for letters and for the punctuation marks no other script uses.

Armenian punctuation is written inside the word. The question mark ՞ sits over the stressed vowel of the word the question is about, as in Ինչպե՞ս ես (How are you?), and the exclamation mark ՜ and emphasis mark ՛ work the same way. The full stop is ։, which looks like a colon, and ՝ marks a pause. Armenian also has no single letter for the vowel u: it is written with two letters, ո and ւ, as in տուն (house). And և, a ligature of ե and ւ, is both a letter and the everyday word for “and”.

Many Armenians write Armenian in Latin letters because the keyboard is missing. On a phone or laptop set up for English or Russian, a message like “Barev, inchpes es?” is quicker than finding an Armenian layout, and it has become a habit in chats. It is readable, but it is not Armenian spelling, and search engines and spell-checkers do not treat it as Armenian. The layout question is also real: Eastern and Western Armenian keyboards put letters in different places, and the phonetic layout on this page, where ա sits on A, ս on S and ք on Q, is the one most people can use without learning anything new. Ethnologue counts about 3.8 million speakers of Eastern Armenian and 1.58 million of Western Armenian, with the largest Western communities in Lebanon, the United States, Georgia and France.
